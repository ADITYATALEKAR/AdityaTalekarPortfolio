01 — OverviewThree forecasting problems, one transferable architecture.

Grid operators balance supply and demand hour by hour, and renewables make both sides harder to predict. EnergyNexus builds three forecasters — demand, electricity price and wind generation — at 1, 6 and 24-hour horizons, and asks whether an architecture designed for one target transfers to another.

02 — DataWhen the data is locked away, build it.

Detailed city-scale energy data sits behind government restrictions, privacy rules and corporate confidentiality. Rather than settle for fragments, I designed a synthetic data generator modelled on London — 9.5 million people, 22 GW peak demand, 3.5 GW of renewable capacity — and paired it with real German renewable data for the wind model.

The synthetic London generator

01

Weather that behaves like weather

Seasonal and daily temperature cycles with autoregressive persistence (Tt = 0.95·Tt−1 + noise); wind speed and solar irradiance with their own seasonal and diurnal shapes.

02

Physically grounded generation

Wind follows a turbine power curve (cut-in, rated and cut-out speeds, cubic in between); solar output is de-rated for heat and for panel degradation over ten years.

03

Six demand sectors

Residential, commercial, industrial, transport, public services and street lighting, shaped by an hourly profile plus heating and cooling load — then expanded across 12 boroughs with their own characteristics (87,600 → 1,051,200 rows).

04

A price-formation model

Price = base £45/MWh + demand pressure + renewable share + time-of-day + gas + carbon + volatility — producing a realistic 26.4% coefficient of variation.

Synthetic London10 years hourly (2014–2023), 12 boroughs, 125 engineered features: 27 lags (1–168 h), 36 rolling windows, 15 economic, 18 weather, 11 demand-sector, 6 momentum and 12 temporal encodings. Each model trains on a continuous 50,000-hour window with a chronological 70/20/10 split.
German windReal 2016 hourly data — 8,711 records from Open Power System Data plus a 256-station weather network, with wind speed at 2 m, 10 m and 50 m and surface roughness as inputs.

03 — ModelsMulti-scale convolution, memory and attention.

The demand network (830,963 parameters). Parallel kernels see short, medium and daily-scale patterns at once.

01

Demand — CNN + BiLSTM + attention

Three parallel convolutions (kernels 3, 7 and 15) capture patterns at different time scales; stacked bidirectional LSTMs model sequence; multi-head attention lets the network weigh the hours that matter. Trained with Huber loss, gradient clipping, early stopping and learning-rate decay.

02

Price — transfer the architecture

Electricity price is far noisier than demand. I reused the demand architecture with heavier dropout and a blended Huber + MAE loss to test whether its inductive biases transfer.

03

Wind — wavelets, then a lean LSTM

Wind is non-stationary and spiky. A Daubechies-3 wavelet decomposition separates the signal into frequency bands before a compact two-layer LSTM (54K parameters) — small model, clean features.

Demand MAPE stayed between 3.9% and 4.6% across horizons — the 6-hour horizon was strongest, which suits medium-term grid planning. The wind model reached an index of agreement of 0.983 and a median absolute error of 1,431 MW.

Error analysis

The worst 5% of wind predictions (66 cases) cluster in high-wind periods, where the model over-estimates by ~15.5% on average — a systematic bias that post-processing calibration could correct. Finding the failure mode mattered more than the headline R².

05 — ReflectionLimits, and what comes next.

The dissertation is explicit about its limits: the London models are validated on synthetic data, and the wind model's residuals are autocorrelated (Durbin-Watson 0.057), suggesting that wavelet preprocessing can disrupt the temporal patterns an LSTM relies on. With the hindsight of a year building production AI, I'd add:

  • Real-world validation through a utility or grid-operator partnership — and use it to calibrate the synthetic generator itself.
  • Probabilistic forecasts — prediction intervals, not just point estimates, especially for wind extremes.
  • Forecast wind from history. The current model maps same-hour weather to generation; a true forecast should use only past observations or weather forecasts.
  • Baselines on every chart — persistence, seasonal-naive and gradient boosting — so every R² has a reference point.
Takeaway

Architecture transfer is real — the demand network carried to price with minimal changes — but the data and the evaluation protocol decide whether a number means anything. That lesson shaped everything I've built since.
